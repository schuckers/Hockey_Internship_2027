# Hockey_Remove_Internship_2027

﻿### Spring 2027 Hockey Research Remote Internship

This page provides information about an *unpaid* research remote internship for the Spring 2027 semester at the University of North Carolina at Charlotte.  
The remote internship is sponsored by the [Los Angeles Kings](https://www.nhl.com/kings/). 
Students selected for this credit bearing remote internship will be mentored by Prof. Michael Schuckers and by members of the distinguished hockey analytics staff of the LA Kings. 
This is a tremendous opportunity to work on significant hockey-related questions while receiving advice and guidance from working professionals in sports analytics.  

Students will be selected based upon an application. Selected students will be provided with access to the relevant data by the Kings, no later than January 5, 2027. 
Students will meet weekly with Prof. Schuckers and approximately bi-weekly or as necessary with Kings staff.  
Neither the work that is part of this remote internship, nor the data provided by the Kings will be made public without the written consent of the Kings. 
At the end of the remote internship, students will create a summary of their work that will be presented to the Kings.  
Undergraduate students will receive three credit hours of DTSC 3400 course credit for this remote internship, while graduate students will receive DTSC 6400 credit.  

## Remote Internship Proposal
There are two focus areas for these remote internships: 1) prospect evaluation and 2) transactions.  These are described below.  
As part of the application students will submit a one-page description of the work they would like to do in one of these two areas.  
That description should include the goals and motivation for the analysis as well as the data and methods to be used.  
We value originality and creativity in these proposals, so the example below of previous work should not be considered limiting.

### Prospect Evaluation
 Projects in this area focus on what we can learn about draft prospects, both before and after the draft. Previous work in this area has largely focused on the pre-draft period, predicting who will play in the NHL or where a player will be drafted. We are especially interested in what happens after the draft, and proposals that explore post-draft trends are encouraged.
Example questions:
- Before the draft

  - Are there skills and traits, whether in statistics or scouting report evaluations, that are predictive of NHL success?
    Does missing time in your draft year (assumed to be due to injury) hurt your draft position and your chance of making the NHL?

- After the draft

  - Are there indications in a player’s statistical profile after he is drafted that he will over- or underperform his draft position? Can predictions improve as more information becomes available one, two, or three years out from the draft, particularly for players who have yet to reach the NHL?
  - Can a player’s trajectory after the draft (based on ice time, points, etc.) be tracked to measure whether he is on track to meet, fall short of, or exceed expectations?
  - At what point should a team be concerned about a player underperforming expectations? This could be years before an NHL debut (based on performance outside the NHL) or a number of games into his NHL career (based on performance once he is there).
  - Should franchises be cautious about overreacting to early-career struggles for players who make the NHL? Do players tend to end up close to the value expected from their draft slot, even if they start slowly?

### Transactions
For this focus area, students will be provided with a list of all NHL transactions (trades, contract signings, waiver claims, etc.), along with contractual data and basic biographical and statistical information. Work should center on one question: how does the NHL market value its assets, and what causes that value to change?
An “asset” can be a current player, a prospect, a draft pick, or cap space. Because these are so different, a strong proposal will start by defining a market value metric that puts them on a common scale. How you build that metric is part of the project, and you should justify your choice.
Example questions:
- Valuing a player
  
  - What is the market value of a player given his position, age, experience, current-season and career statistics, and contract?
  - How has that valuation changed over time? Can you model what the same player would have been worth 5 or 10 years ago?
  - Are there systematic trends by age, contract length, or contract structure?

- Context-dependent valuation
  
  - How do teams value assets differently depending on where they are in the standings? Consider playoff position, how comfortable that position is, and how early or late in the season it is.
  - Do contenders prefer current players while non-contenders prefer futures (prospects and picks)?
  - Do teams overpay for an asset that fits a current need, relative to what we would expect the market to pay? How would you measure “expected”?

- Cap environment

  - How much does a player’s cap hit as a percentage of the cap influence his value over time?
  - Did the flat cap during COVID penalize players with larger relative cap hits more heavily?
